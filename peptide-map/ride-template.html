Which peptide, and how does it reach you?

Pick one at the counter. The doors open, the cart climbs the track that today's US rules lay down for it through the Lebanon Valley in October, four windows show the work at each step and whether the rules permit it, and the plate at the end gives you the facts with their dates and sources. About a minute.

The weather on the ride follows the peptide's standing: clear skies for an approved product, overcast where compounding is tolerated and human trials exist, rain and lightning where there is no approval and only grey-market supply, a storm where the human evidence is thin, a blizzard with hail where the evidence is laboratory and animal work only.     How to read the ride

    Four windows open in the corners as you climb, one per station, and show the work: the raw source, purifying and testing, fill and finish, dispensing. The banner over each station carries its words. Green means the rules permit the step, amber means with conditions, red means the gate is closed. A cart that stops at a gate, or gets shunted onto the research-use-only siding, is the answer.

    Where the facts come from

    Every status and date on the plate is a row in the federal register behind this tool: FDA's lists and letters, the Federal Register, Regulations.gov, the courts. The map holds the register, the 503B facilities and the documented supply routes; the glossary holds the words.

    What it is not

    Not legal advice, and not a statement that any peptide is safe or works. "Tolerated" means FDA or HHS says it does not intend to act while conditions are met. It is never approval. The hills are the Pennsylvania countryside the ride is set in; the equipment in the windows is drawn to type, not from any named plant.
